§1 The Problem with Trending Data

Most data analysts reach for linear regression as a first tool. But regression assumes that your variables fluctuate around a stable mean. Many real-world series — brand awareness, consumer confidence, revenue — don't do that. They drift. They have a unit root: a persistent upward or downward trend with no natural home to return to.

When you regress one drifting series on another, you get a spurious regression. The R² looks great. The t-statistics look significant. But the relationship is a statistical artefact — both series happened to drift in the same direction over your sample period. The model will give you completely different answers on the next dataset.

The fix is not to difference the data and run VAR on Δy — you lose all information about the long-run level relationship. The fix is a Vector Error Correction Model, which simultaneously models the short-run dynamics and the long-run equilibrium.

The classic diagnostic Run OLS on two I(1) series. If the residuals look like a random walk (ADF fails to reject), the regression is spurious — stop and reach for VECM.
Under the hood — unit roots and integration order

A series yt has a unit root if it can be written as yt = yt-1 + εt. Repeated substitution shows the variance grows without bound: Var(yt) = t·σ². The series is called integrated of order 1, written I(1).

The standard test is the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, which tests H0: unit root present. Failure to reject → the series is I(1) and you should not run OLS on it in levels.

Δyt = ρ·yt-1 + Σ φi·Δyt-i + εt     H0: ρ = 0

A VAR in differences (ΔVAR) is correctly specified but throws away the long-run information encoded in the level relationship between variables. Engle and Granger (1987) proved that if two I(1) series are cointegrated, they must have an error-correction representation — VECM is that representation.

§2 Cointegration: the Long-Run Tether

Two I(1) series are cointegrated if a linear combination of them is stationary — if there exists a vector β such that β'yₜ ~ I(0). Intuitively, the series share a common stochastic trend. They can drift apart temporarily, but a force pulls them back together.

Think of a dog on a long lead walking with its owner. Both the dog and the owner follow roughly random paths — neither is predictable moment-to-moment. But the distance between them is bounded: when the lead goes taut, the dog gets pulled back. The lead is the cointegrating relationship.

The cointegrating relationship is the long-run equilibrium equation. When the system drifts away from it — when the gap opens up — the VECM's error-correction term kicks in and pulls the variables back toward balance.

Marketing interpretation Brand spend and organic sales are cointegrated: they share a common trend (the growth of the business). The cointegrating residual measures how far sales have deviated from the level implied by brand investment. The error-correction term captures the long-run reversion — the tail that standard MMMs miss entirely.
Under the hood — the Granger representation theorem

Engle and Granger (1987) proved: if yt ~ I(1) and there exists β such that β'yt ~ I(0), then yt has an error-correction representation:

Δyt = α·β'yt-1 + lagged differences + εt

This is a two-way result. Cointegration implies error correction, and error correction implies cointegration. You cannot have one without the other. This is why differencing and running a VAR loses information — you're throwing away the α·β'yt-1 term that encodes the long-run pull.

The number of linearly independent cointegrating vectors is the cointegration rank r, where 0 ≤ r ≤ K. If r = 0, no long-run relationships exist. If r = K, all series are stationary (shouldn't be using VECM). Typically 1 ≤ r ≤ K-1.

Under the hood — the Granger-Johansen parametrisation

The key identity is that the K×K matrix Π = αβ′ has rank r. This means Π is not full-rank — it cannot be inverted — which is what allows the system to have I(1) components despite having an error-correction mechanism.

Π = αβ′    rank(Π) = r    α, β ∈ ℝK×r

The decomposition Π = αβ′ is not unique: for any invertible r×r matrix H, we can write Π = (αH⁻¹)(Hβ′). This is the identification problem — we must impose r² normalisation restrictions on β to pin down a unique solution. The Johansen normalisation pins the top r×r block of β to Ir.

§4 β — The Long-Run Equilibrium

β is the matrix of cointegrating vectors. Each column of β defines one long-run equilibrium relationship among the K variables. With r=1 and K=3, β is a 3×1 vector like [1, -0.8, -0.2]′ — meaning organic_sales - 0.8·awareness - 0.2·consideration = 0 in the long run.

You can read β as the long-run equilibrium equation. If you know awareness and consideration, β tells you where organic sales should be. When actual sales deviate from this level, the error-correction mechanism in α kicks in to close the gap.

Identification: pinning down a unique β

β is not unique out of the box. If β is a cointegrating vector, so is 2β, -β, or any rotation of β. We must impose normalisation restrictions to pick a unique solution. The standard approach (Johansen normalisation) pins the top r×r block of β to an identity matrix:

β = [ Ir ; βfree ]′    — the first variable in each relation is the "numeraire"

In practice this means: if r=1 and organic_sales is listed first, the cointegrating vector reads "organic_sales = β₂·awareness + β₃·consideration + …" — organic sales is the normalising variable, and the other β entries are its long-run elasticities with respect to the brand metrics.

Under the hood — why β is not identified without restrictions

The identification problem comes from the rotation invariance of Π = αβ′. For any invertible r×r matrix H:

Π = αβ′ = (αH⁻¹)(Hβ′)

So (α*, β*) = (αH⁻¹, βH′) gives the same Π as (α, β). There are r² free parameters in H, so we need r² normalisation restrictions. The Johansen normalisation imposes β[1:r, :] = Ir, which is r² constraints (r entries per row × r rows, each set to a known value).

In the Bayesian model, βfree ~ Normal(0, 5) as a prior on the lower (K-r)×r block, with the top Ir block fixed as a constant (not a random variable). This prevents the sampler from exploring the rotation-equivalent solutions that would create multimodality in the posterior.

§5 α — Speed of Adjustment

α is the adjustment matrix. Each row of α corresponds to one variable; each column corresponds to one cointegrating relationship. The entry αij tells you: when cointegrating relationship j deviates from zero by one unit, how much does variable i change in the next period to correct it?

The sign and magnitude of α are economically interpretable:

  • Negative αi — variable i is a follower. When the system goes out of equilibrium, it adjusts back. Large negative α means fast adjustment.
  • Near-zero αi — variable i is a driver. It doesn't respond to disequilibrium; instead it pushes other variables around.
  • Positive αi — variable i amplifies deviations rather than correcting them (unusual; may indicate misspecification).
Brand marketing interpretation If organic_sales has α = -0.3, it corrects 30% of any deviation from the brand equity equilibrium each period. After 2 periods, two thirds of the deviation is closed. A large negative α on sales is a healthy sign — the brand system is self-correcting.
Under the hood — weak exogeneity and block α

A variable is weakly exogenous with respect to the cointegrating parameters if its row in α is zero — it doesn't respond to disequilibrium, it only drives other variables. In brand marketing, brand spend is typically weakly exogenous (it's set by the business, not adjusted in response to brand equity gaps), so it enters as an exogenous regressor rather than an endogenous VECM variable.

Formal test: Likelihood ratio test of H0: αi = 0. In the Bayesian setting, the posterior of αi concentrated near zero achieves the same effect without a hard exclusion restriction.

§6 Γ — Short-Run Dynamics

The Γ matrices capture transient effects — how changes in the system last period ripple through to this period, above and beyond the long-run error-correction pull. If organic sales jumped last week, Γ tells you how much of that jump is still felt this week.

Unlike β (which is permanent) and α (which governs the persistence of deviations), Γ effects decay. A system with only error correction and no short-run dynamics (Γ = 0) has a very clean impulse response: the shock hits, and the adjustment is monotone. Real marketing data usually has richer short-run structure.

How many lags? The horseshoe answer

With K=4 variables and k lags, the Γ block has K²k = 16k parameters. Setting k too low misses real dynamics; setting it too high over-fits. The Bayesian solution is to set k generously (k=2 or 3) and put a horseshoe prior on Γ — this shrinks irrelevant entries toward zero automatically, without making a hard include/exclude decision.

Under the hood — the regularised horseshoe (Piironen & Vehtari 2017)

The regularised horseshoe places a local-global scale mixture on each Γ entry:

Γij ~ Normal(0, τ·λ̃ij)    λ̃ij = c·λij / sqrt(c² + τ²·λ²ij)
λij ~ Half-Cauchy(1)    (local scale — per-entry)
τ ~ Half-Cauchy(τ₀)    (global scale — overall sparsity)
c² ~ Inv-Gamma(ν/2, ν·s²/2)    (slab width — caps large effects)

The global scale τ controls overall sparsity — small τ pushes everything toward zero. The local scale λij allows individual entries to escape shrinkage via the heavy Cauchy tails. The slab width c caps the local scales so the prior is proper and sampling is stable. Together they achieve near-zero shrinkage on true zeros and near-unshrunken estimates on true signals.

§7 Rank Selection — How Many Equilibria?

Before fitting the model, you need to decide on the cointegration rank r — how many long-run equilibrium relationships exist in your system. This is a structural decision: r=1 means one equilibrium force holds the system together, r=2 means two independent equilibria.

The standard tool is the Johansen trace test. It tests a sequence of null hypotheses from r=0 upward, stopping when the test first fails to reject.

The test gives a data-driven starting point, but domain knowledge can override it. If theory strongly suggests one equilibrium (e.g. brand spend and organic sales share a common growth trend), r=1 is defensible even if the test is borderline.

Under the hood — the trace statistic

The Johansen trace statistic for H0: rank ≤ r is:

LR(r) = -T · Σi=r+1K log(1 - λ̂i)

where λ̂r+1, …, λ̂K are the K-r smallest squared canonical correlations between Δyt and yt-1 after partialling out lagged differences. Under H0, this statistic follows a non-standard distribution (a functional of Brownian motion) tabulated by Johansen (1991) and Osterwald-Lenum (1992). Critical values depend on K-r and the deterministic specification.

§8 Generalised Impulse Response Functions

An impulse response function (IRF) answers the question: "if one variable is shocked by one unit today, what happens to all variables over the next N periods?" This is the key output for brand marketing — it quantifies the long-run tail of a brand spend shock on organic sales.

The standard IRF (Cholesky) requires you to specify a causal ordering: which variables affect which contemporaneously. For a brand system (awareness ↔ consideration ↔ sales, with feedback loops in all directions), any fixed ordering is wrong. Generalised IRFs (GIRF) solve this — they are order-invariant, computing the average effect across all possible orderings.

The I(1) nature of the system means the GIRF does not decay to zero. A permanent shock to brand spend shifts the long-run equilibrium, and organic sales converge to a new, higher level. This is the economically meaningful output — it quantifies the brand equity that accumulates from sustained investment.

Under the hood — GIRF derivation (Pesaran & Shin 1998)

First, convert the VECM to its levels VAR(p) representation with p = k+1:

A₁ = I + αβ′ + Γ₁    Aj = Γj - Γj-1 (j=2..k)    Ak+1 = -Γk

Then iterate the companion matrix F (the K(k+1) × K(k+1) stacked-variable matrix) to get the impulse response matrices:

Φh = top-left K×K block of Fh

The Cholesky IRF uses a lower-triangular factorisation P of Σ = PP′ and computes OIRh = ΦhP. The GIRF averages over all possible orderings, giving:

GIRFh(i, j) = Φh Σ ej / sqrt(Σjj)

This is equivalent to conditioning on a one-standard-deviation shock to variable j, averaging over the contemporaneous correlation structure. It is fully order-invariant and equals the Cholesky IRF only when Σ is diagonal.

§9 The Bayesian Treatment

The classical (frequentist) VECM produces point estimates of α, β, Γ with asymptotic standard errors. The Bayesian VECM produces full posterior distributions — you get not just "α = -0.3" but a probability distribution over all plausible values of α given the data.

This matters for three reasons in practice:

  • Honest uncertainty in IRFs. Every posterior draw of (α, β, Γ, Σ) produces one IRF curve. The fan chart in §8 is the distribution over all those curves — credible intervals, not confidence intervals.
  • Small samples. Marketing data is often 3–5 years of monthly observations (36–60 rows). Classical inference breaks down; Bayesian priors regularise the model and prevent over-fitting.
  • Principled sparsity. The horseshoe prior on Γ is inexpressible in classical VECM. It's a natural Bayesian construct.
Under the hood — MCMC, HMC, and convergence diagnostics

The posterior is computed via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) with the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), implemented in PyMC. HMC uses gradient information to propose efficient moves through parameter space, making it far more effective than random-walk Metropolis for high-dimensional, correlated posteriors.

Key convergence diagnostics:

R̂ (Gelman-Rubin): ratio of between-chain to within-chain variance. R̂ < 1.01 indicates convergence. Values > 1.05 mean chains haven't mixed — increase draws or tune.

ESS (Effective Sample Size): how many independent draws your correlated chain is worth. ESS < 400 means poor coverage of the posterior; increase draws.

Divergences: HMC proposals rejected due to energy conservation violations. A few is expected; many divergences indicate the posterior has difficult geometry (often the horseshoe funnel). Fix: increase target_accept toward 0.95.

§10 Brand Marketing Application

The full motivation for this package is the brand marketing measurement problem. Standard Marketing Mix Models (MMMs) attribute revenue to media channels using short-window adstock transformations. Brand investment — TV, OOH, digital brand — has effects that last years, not weeks. Standard MMM misses the long-run tail entirely.

Why VECM beats MMM for brand measurement

An MMM with adstock decay models brand effects as a geometric series that fades within 4–8 weeks. A VECM with cointegration captures the fact that a sustained brand investment shifts the long-run equilibrium — the business settles at a permanently higher level of organic sales. The GIRF shows this: the response curve doesn't decay to zero. The total long-run area under the GIRF curve is the true economic value of the brand investment.
