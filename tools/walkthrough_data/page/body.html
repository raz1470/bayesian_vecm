How Long Does Brand Last?

An MMM counts a few weeks of brand effect. A Bayesian VECM follows the rest: from spend to awareness, consideration and organic sales, over years.

1 · The problem 2 · What a VECM is 3 · The data 4 · Tests before fitting 5 · The fit 6 · The awareness effect 7 · Which links exist 8 · A campaign 9 · Return per channel 10 · Your own data 11 · Questions

Brand teams have long argued that brand advertising pays back over years, not weeks. Les Binet and Peter Field put evidence behind that in The Long and the Short of It (IPA, 2013). Activation drives short sales spikes. Brand building grows slowly and lasts much longer.

Marketing Mix Models find the long part hard to see. This article fits a Bayesian Vector Error Correction Model (VECM) to the sales an MMM leaves in its baseline. It follows the approach of Cain (2022), who modelled awareness, consideration and base sales together to measure short and long-term marketing effects.

We use simulated data, so every estimate can be checked against the truth. In our simulation £1 of brand spend brings back £2.00 within a year and £11.34 within five. Only 18% of that arrives in the first year.

1 · The problem

An MMM stops counting after a few weeks

An MMM carries each week's spend forward with adstock. A typical decay is gone within a few weeks. Whatever brand does after that ends up in the baseline, where the model reads it as sales that would have happened anyway.

Organic sales: what the MMM leaves behind

The VECM does not replace the MMM. It works on the MMM's output. We take total sales and subtract what the MMM attributes to performance media:

organic sales = total sales − performance media contribution (from the MMM)

Performance media is the short-run activation the MMM measures well: paid search, paid social and affiliates. What is left is the target of this article. It holds the slow-moving demand that brand builds, along with everything else the MMM could not explain.

Brand channels stay in organic sales. Their effect is what we want to measure, so removing the MMM's estimate of it would remove part of the answer. Two consequences follow. Errors in the MMM's performance estimates flow into organic sales. And if your MMM baseline still holds seasonality, take it out or add seasonal terms as drivers.

Two shapes for the same burst

An illustration, not an estimate. By week 10 the adstock response is down to 3% of its peak. The error-correction response is 72% of the way to a new level and stays there.

With a decay of 0.7 the adstock effect halves about every two weeks. Summed over every week it is worth about three weeks of the first week's effect.

The VECM tells a different story. A brand burst raises awareness, and awareness keeps its gain. Consideration and sales now sit below where the new awareness level says they should be. Each week they close part of that gap until they reach a higher level. Under adstock the total stops growing after a few weeks. Under error correction every week adds more.

2 · What a VECM is

A model for series that trend together

Awareness, consideration and organic sales all trend over years. That breaks ordinary regression, and it is the reason a VECM exists.

Trending series fool regression

A series with a unit root has no level to return to. Each week is last week plus a shock: yt = yt−1 + εt. Its variance grows with time. A series like this is called integrated of order one, written I(1). Its weekly change is stationary, written I(0).

Regress one I(1) series on another and you will often find a strong link where none exists. This is spurious regression (Granger and Newbold 1974). The chart shows two random walks we generated independently of each other.

Two unrelated random walks
Generated independently, 260 weeks each
Walk A Walk B

Regressing A on B gives an R² of 0.49 and a t-statistic of 15.8. Neither series has anything to do with the other.

This pair is not a rare case. We repeated the exercise on 1,000 pairs of independent random walks. In 86% of them the slope came out significant at the 5% level, where a valid test would give 5%.

Taking weekly changes fixes the test but throws away the levels. For brand that is the part we care about: whether sales settle at a higher level after awareness rises.

Cointegration: trending, but tied together

Some I(1) series are tied to each other. Each one wanders but some combination of them does not. That combination is stationary, and the series are cointegrated (Engle and Granger 1987). Think of a dog on a long lead. Neither path is predictable, but the distance between them is bounded.

The cointegration test of Engle and Granger shows the difference. For the two random walks above it finds no tie (p = 0.20). For log organic sales against log consideration in our data it finds one (p < 0.001). The R² of that regression is 0.99, and this time it means something.

The equation

Engle and Granger also proved that cointegrated series can always be written in error correction form. For K series stacked in a vector yt, the VECM is:

Δyt = αβ′yt−1 + ΓΔyt−1 + Bxt + c + εt,    εt ~ N(0, Σ)

The weekly change in each series has four sources. The first term is the pull back towards the long run. The second is momentum from last week's changes. The third is the drivers and the fourth is noise.

Each term, with its size in our model (3 brand metrics, 5 drivers)
TermSizeWhat it holds
yt3Log levels of organic sales, awareness and consideration
β3 × 2The long-run relationships. β′yt−1 is how far each one was from holding last week.
α3 × 2Adjustment speeds: how much of each gap each series closes per week

B3 × 5Same-week effects of the drivers: channels and macro series
c3A constant, which lets the series drift upwards
Σ3 × 3Covariance of the weekly shocks. Off-diagonal terms hold the same-week links.

The rank: how many long-run relationships

The matrix Π = αβ′ is K × K, but its rank r is what matters:

  • r = 0. No long-run relationship. The model is a VAR in weekly changes.
  • 0 < r < K. There are r long-run relationships and K − r shared trends. This is the VECM case.
  • r = K. Every series is stationary. Use a VAR in levels instead.

Our data has K = 3 and r = 2: sales tie to consideration and consideration ties to awareness. That leaves one shared trend. It belongs to awareness, which is why a rise in awareness carries through to sales for good.

Reading α and β

β is not unique on its own. Any rescaling of a relationship describes the same tie, so the model fixes the top r × r block of β to the identity. Each relationship is then anchored on one series. Ours read:

gap 1 = sales − β1 × consideration     gap 2 = awareness − β2 × consideration

The series are in logs, so β1 and β2 are long-run elasticities. Each entry of α says how a series responds when a gap opens. Its sign depends on which side of the relationship the series sits:

  • Sales sit on the left of gap 1. When sales are above their level a negative α pulls them down.
  • Consideration sits on the right of gap 2. When awareness runs ahead a positive α pulls consideration up.
  • An α near zero in every gap means the series does not adjust. It is weakly exogenous: it leads and the others follow.

A series that closes a share a of its gap each week has a half-life of about ln(0.5) / ln(1 − a) weeks, on its own. In the full system the gaps interact, so read the impulse responses for the actual path.

The Bayesian part

A classical VECM gives point estimates and large-sample standard errors. The Bayesian version gives a posterior for every parameter. The returns we care about are non-linear functions of every parameter. Computing them once per posterior draw carries all of that uncertainty through to the £ figure. The priors are weakly informative:

4 · Tests before fitting

Three trending series and two long-run relationships

Two decisions come before the fit. Which series trend and which are stationary. And how many long-run relationships tie the trending ones together.

Step 1: Which series trend?

check_stationarity runs an Augmented Dickey-Fuller test on each series and on its weekly change. The null hypothesis is a unit root, so a small p-value means stationary. It tests the change first: a series that is still trending after one difference needs differencing before it goes in.

This matches how the data was built. On real data the test has low power. A stationary series that returns to its average slowly can look like a trend, especially in a short sample.

Stationarity is not the only condition for a driver. A driver must not respond to the brand metrics. If budgets follow last month's sales, spend carries that feedback and its estimated effect absorbs it. A Granger test on the drivers before modelling is the check for this. It is separate from the tests on the fitted model in section 7.

Step 2: How many long-run relationships?

select_coint_rank runs the Johansen trace test. It tests r ≤ 0, then r ≤ 1 and so on, and stops at the first null it cannot reject.

The test recommends two, which is the true rank. It is a guide and not a ruling. Across 400 simulated datasets from the same process it returns the true rank about 80% of the time. If you have a good reason to expect a given number of relationships, set it yourself.

5 · The fit

The model recovers the funnel and forecasts a year it has not seen

# endog: log levels of the three brand metrics. exog: the five drivers.
model = BayesianVECM(k_ar_diff=1, coint_rank=2, deterministic="co")
model.fit(endog, exog=exog, draws=1000, tune=1000, chains=2, target_accept=0.9)

k_ar_diff=1 gives one lag of weekly changes. deterministic="co" puts a constant outside the long-run relationships, which lets the series drift upwards.

Step 3: Check the fit in the sample

The model explains weekly changes, so that is where we measure fit. A model of levels would score close to 1 on any trending series. The residuals should carry no pattern the model missed.

With your own data, autocorrelation in the residuals is the one to act on. Add a lag and refit.

Step 4: Forecast a year the model has not seen

The strongest check is out of sample. We refit on the first four years and forecast 2025 with the drivers as they actually were. The benchmark is a random walk with drift: the last value plus the average weekly growth.

6 · The awareness effect

A rise in awareness lifts sales for good

Suppose awareness jumps unexpectedly this week. An impulse response function traces what happens to every series over the following weeks, with an interval at every week. The jump here is one standard deviation of awareness's weekly shock.

Response to a surprise rise in awareness
% change from where each series would otherwise be, with 89% interval
Organic sales Awareness Consideration

None of the three responses fades. Sales move 0.58% in the first week, peak at 1.16% in week 4 and settle 1.08% higher (0.94% to 1.24%).

The response does not decay because awareness carries the system's one shared trend. Below, the sum of the weekly sales responses is set against an adstock curve with decay 0.7, scaled to match over the first four weeks.

Total sales response, VECM against adstock
Sum of the weekly % uplifts, both matched at week 4
VECM, with 89% interval Adstock, decay 0.7

The two agree for a month. By week 52 the VECM's total is 9.7 times the adstock's.

The adstock curve is our choice and not a fitted MMM. A slower decay would close some of the gap and a longer window would widen it. Any adstock total flattens out. The VECM's total keeps growing for as long as sales stay higher.

Why a generalised impulse response?

The shocks are correlated within a week, so a surprise in awareness usually comes with some surprise in the others. An impulse response has to decide who moves whom in the same week.

The VECM first converts to a VAR in levels with lag matrices A1 = I + αβ′ + Γ and A2 = −Γ. Iterating it gives the response matrices Φh at each week h. Two ways to shock series j:

  • Cholesky. ΦhP, where Σ = PP′ is lower triangular. Each series can move the ones after it in the same week but not the ones before. The order is your assumption.
  • Generalised (GIRF). ΦhΣej / √σjj (Pesaran and Shin 1998). The other series move by their expected same-week response to series j.

The GIRF for series j equals the Cholesky response with j placed first. So it is not free of assumptions: it treats the shocked series as leading within the week. That fits awareness. Cain (2022) estimated these same-week links on real brand data and found awareness was the only metric that moved the others within the week.

The choice barely matters here. With sales ordered first instead, the week-52 response is 1.01% (0.87% to 1.19%) against 1.08%. For shocks further down the funnel an explicit ordering is the safer read.

irf = model.irf(steps=52, method="girf")
irf_sales_first = model.irf(steps=52, method="cholesky")  # column order

7 · Which links exist

The fitted model finds every true link and no false ones

Granger causality asks whether one series helps predict another. In a VECM there are two routes. Last week's change can carry through Γ, and the level can pull through αβ′. A test on weekly changes alone sees only the first. So granger_causality reads both routes from the fitted posterior.

Each link gets an evidence score between 0 and 1. It is the highest credible level at which zero lies outside the posterior of the effect. A score of 0.95 or more counts as a link. The score says how sure we are that a link exists and not how big it is.

8 · A campaign

Six months of extra spend, still paying at week 52

Impulse responses describe surprises, but spend is planned. So we run the fitted model forward for a year twice. The baseline holds every driver at its average. The campaign raises brand spend by one standard deviation for 26 weeks. Both runs share the same random shocks, so the difference is the spend alone.

10 · Your own data

Three steps from your data to a return per channel

The guide notebook runs everything in this article. One cell loads the data. Swap it for your own and the rest runs as it is.

1

Prepare

Build organic sales from your MMM: total sales minus the performance media contribution. Use weekly data with at least two to three years of history. Take logs of the three brand metrics. Put spend in £1,000s and reach in 100,000s, then subtract each driver's average. Check that budgets do not follow sales, and check how the brand tracker is built. A rolling average smooths away the timing the model learns from.

2

Test and fit

Run the stationarity and rank tests, then fit with one or two lags. Check the sampler before reading anything. Plot the gaps and check they return to zero. Check the residuals for autocorrelation. Hold back the last year and compare the forecast with a drift benchmark.

3

Read

Start with the response to an awareness surprise: does the sales uplift last? Then the evidence tables: does the funnel run the way you expect? Then the return per channel. Set a £ cost per unit for each reach channel so all of them come out per £1.

11 · Questions

Frequently asked questions

Why not just use a longer adstock in my MMM?

A longer adstock still decays to zero, just more slowly. You also have to pick the decay, and spend data rarely pins it down. A VECM lets the data say whether sales settle at a new level and ties that level to brand metrics you already track.

Why not a VAR in weekly changes?

It is a valid model but it drops the αβ′ term. A shock can still shift levels for good, but nothing ties sales back to awareness. The long-run elasticities and the speed at which gaps close are exactly what that term holds.

Is this causal?

The evidence tables are statements about prediction. The campaign and the return per channel read the driver effects as causal. That holds only if spend does not respond to the brand metrics or to sales.

What about saturation?

The model is linear in each driver. Its returns are marginal returns near current spend. Transform spend before fitting to see diminishing returns, and until then do not use the returns to set a budget ceiling.

What if spend takes a week or two to move awareness?

Drivers act in the same week. A delayed effect can go in as extra columns of spend lagged by one or two weeks. The total effect of a pulse is then the sum of each column's response shifted by its lag.

Why no variance decomposition?

The package has one: model.fevd() splits forecast uncertainty by source. It covers shocks to the brand metrics only, so the channels never appear in it. For a budget question the impulse responses and the return per channel say more.

How much data do I need?

The long-run part is learned from how the series move together over years, so more history helps. With five years of weekly data the rank test finds the true rank about 80% of the time. With less, expect wider intervals and lean more on what you know about your funnel.

Brand works through awareness and consideration, and the gains hold sales up for years. On data where we know the truth, a Bayesian VECM recovers that funnel and puts 82% of brand's five-year return after the first year.
